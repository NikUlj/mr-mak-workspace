**Staging brings income forward, but increases total work.** At the middle rates, horror starts selling about 11.6 months earlier at 21 h/week, yet finishes about 4.3 months later; mech starts about 10.4 months earlier and finishes about 5.4 months later. Those are consequences of these first-release boundaries and support assumptions, not universal properties of EA. At 31 h/week the respective gaps to completion are shorter, so more later-release revenue can enter the first paid-year window.

## Sensitivities that could change the schedule

These change one assumption at a time, keeping the larger content promise. Halving all asset hours is an intentionally aggressive sensitivity, not an AI forecast. The 25% systems saving includes the non-asset risk reserve.

| Change | Horror release, months | Mech release, months | Scrapline release, months |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Middle, 21 h/week | 37.3 | 44.1 | 10.6 |
| 31 h/week throughout | 25.3 | 29.9 | 7.2 |
| 21 -> 31 after six months | 27.2 | 31.8 | 9.1 |
| Only 90% of stated hours available | 41.5 | 49.0 | 11.7 |
| Asset hours down 25% | 32.1 | 38.7 | 9.4 |
| Asset hours down 50% | 26.9 | 33.2 | 8.3 |
| Other work down 25% | 33.2 | 38.6 | 9.1 |
| Three-month pre-release pause | 40.3 | 47.1 | 13.6 |

Existing-code credit is an evidence gate: even hypothetical accepted savings of 40 horror hours or 120 mech hours would move a 21-hour schedule only about 1.9 or 5.7 weeks. Those example credits are **not** measured or applied. Savings from a much faster end-to-end pipeline could be larger, which is why the benchmark matters.

The owner rejected these smaller experiences as the main target. This coarse reduction re-costs the [archived compact scopes](game-scopes-compact-v1.md) at the same base unit rates, with explicit reduced quantities/system fractions in the input JSON. It is not a recommendation to substitute them. Their reduced rosters need their own appeal test.
