**Research plan | 21 October 2026**

**Outcome:** Recommend which of the three ideas to pursue, at a specific scope,
based on the owner's asset-production limits, remaining development effort,
commercial evidence, and personal interest.

**Agreed revision, 23 October 2026:** Earning potential leads the next decision.
Step 6 now selects a concept for a representative prototype, provisionally,
before a full numerical estimate rebuild. The owner does not know the duration
of most tasks and should not have to supply replacement estimates. Keep the

Use prototype observations and player response to revisit the production

This is the execution plan, not a completed feasibility study. The
[Step 1 findings](constraints-and-reuse.md) establish the research baseline;

is deliberately separate from the eventual recommendation.

**Confirmed baseline:** 21 development hours/week now, potentially 31 later;
the owner will create assets personally using AI-assisted 3D and animation
workflows. Both supplied prototypes use Unity. The desired outcome is an early
release with strong financial returns; no fixed deadline, income floor, or AI
spending cap has been specified. Model both schedules without assuming when the
increase in hours happens. RIG Riot is the confirmed direct mech comparison.
The likely modeling tool is **Tripo**. The intended animation route is **video
reference -> GPT agent operating Blender through MCP -> manual corrections in
Cascadeur or Blender -> Unity integration**. Evaluate this specific route first.
Both 3D candidates should use a **simpler low-poly aesthetic**. Personal preference
is **mech first, horror a close second**. The owner has **no previous commercial
game releases or existing launch audience**. These are confirmed constraints,
not findings about which game will earn more.

**Scope correction:** the owner found the initial horror and mech proposals too
small. The horror target is a 4-6-hour campaign in one substantial interconnected
location. The mech target includes both deeper customization and more areas,
enemies, and bosses. Estimate the desired games first; retain the compact
versions as explicitly smaller fallback cases. Exact asset/content quantities
remain proposed inputs, and feasibility at the available hours is unproven.

**Release-route clarification:** the owner is open to Early Access and episodic
releases for the larger games. Compare them with a full launch while retaining
the intended completed scope. No route, episode count, update cadence, or
purchase entitlement is selected. The [release-route cases](game-scopes.md)
separate the first paid offering from the completed game.

## What would make this research useful?

The answer must describe a game the owner could actually finish. Comparing the
sales of Dead Space with a tiny incremental game would not answer that question.
We will compare the intended indie-scale versions of each idea, with their
tradeoffs visible. Do not silently shrink the owner's desired experience to
optimize the schedule. Assess whether each proposed release is commercially and
operationally credible, and show smaller options separately if necessary.
We will report cash outcomes separately from compensation for the owner's time.

## 3. Build a market comparison that includes weak outcomes

**Status: desk market comparison complete; 21 October 2026 data, finalized 22 October locally.**
[Findings](market-findings.md), [29 paid comparables and method](market-comparison.md),
[99 player reviews across 12 games](market-reviews.md), and
[release-strategy evidence](release-strategies.md) are documented. The main set
contains 11 horror, 7 mech (3 especially direct), and 11 incremental releases;
five upcoming titles are separate. US pricing, review filters, EA dates and
comparability limits are explicit. Public finances remain incomplete, particularly
for the closest mech/horror matches; no sales multiplier or success probability
was substituted. No comparable-game playthroughs were performed.

**Output:** A source-linked comparison of approximately 8-12 relevant releases
per candidate, plus a short review analysis. Use fewer direct mech comparables
if the market is sparse and label adjacent games separately.

Start with releases in the preceding 24 months, supplementing with older close
matches and a separate list of upcoming competitors. Record the search dates,
tags/queries, inclusion rules, and excluded mismatches. Search recent-release
lists as well as prominent games. Include games with little traction, ordinary
outcomes, and hits; select for scope and audience similarity before considering
sales. This is a comparison sample, not a random sample of the entire market.

Match the revised target releases first. Label shorter horror games and very
small arena roguelites as fallback/adjacent comparisons where appropriate.
Content scope and asset complexity matter more than sharing a genre tag.

Include relevant Early Access and episodic examples. Record first paid release,
1.0 date or current unfinished status, content available at each observed stage,
price/entitlement changes, update intervals, and completion or stalled-development
evidence where documented. Compare initial chapters with initial chapters and
Early Access stages with similar stages; do not compare a first paid build's
sales directly with another game's complete lifetime. Investigate ongoing support
burden, audience retention, and whether repeated releases actually brought buyers.

For each game, record release and Early Access dates, current status, team and
development history when disclosed, core loop, camera, art style, content scope,
base and discounted price with region/currency, review count and rating, and
evidence of an existing audience, publisher, or creator exposure. Treat unknowns
as unknowns. Separate full games, demos, prologues, and free-to-play titles.

Prefer Steam pages for product facts and developer postmortems for reported
costs, hours, units, and revenue. A publisher name is not a team-size estimate.
Record whether financial evidence is developer-reported or third-party modeled,
what deductions it includes, and which sales window it covers. Use comparable
30/90/365-day windows where evidence exists; otherwise show age-at-observation.
Never substitute lifetime revenue for launch revenue without labeling it.

Review a consistent sample of positive and negative player comments for scope,
repetition, controls, animation, progression, perceived value, and unfinished
content. Record the sampling method and counts; use qualitative themes rather
than claiming those comments measure the whole audience.

Check whether recent incremental interest persists across multiple releases,
whether competition is growing, and which subgenres are actually selling.
Analyze RIG Riot directly and use Granvir as a secondary comparison. Identify
what the competitors validate and where they leave room to differ.
Separate broad horror demand from demand for short third-person action horror.

**Acceptance:** Every important claim has a dated source and a comparability
note. Review counts, wishlists, and concurrent players are not presented as
sales or profit. Any review-to-sales model is a wide, calibrated sensitivity
exercise, not reported revenue. A selected sample cannot establish a genre-wide
probability of success.

Treat any purchased or commissioned alternative as an optional comparison only.

Evaluate the complete AI-assisted pipeline: concept/reference preparation,
generation attempts and rejected outputs, geometry cleanup, retopology or
decimation, UVs/materials, rigging/skin weights, animation generation or motion
transfer, correction, export, Unity integration, and visual acceptance. Count

For Tripo and the specified Blender/Cascadeur workflow, check current official

Estimate credits
and subscriptions separately from hands-on time and waiting time; do not apply
an unsupported blanket AI productivity multiplier. Preserve successful jobs
and source files when later experiments are authorized. No paid generation is
needed for this planning stage.

For animation, measure agent setup/iteration time separately from the owner's
manual correction time. Evaluate a small connected set of clips on the intended

camera. Include round-trip export and skin/bone compatibility when Cascadeur is
used. This is reference-guided authoring; it is not automatically measured motion
capture. A practical benchmark passes only when the owner judges the animation
acceptable in the game and the recorded effort fits the planned content budget.

Calibrate with the existing horror strafe revision before assuming fast
animation delivery: its recorded checks separate successful Blender reimport
from still-pending Unity validation. This is evidence that acceptance has
multiple stages, not a measure of hours per animation.

| Candidate | Work that must be counted |
| --- | --- |
| Horror | Player and creature models, distinct rigs, locomotion and combat clips, aiming/reload/hit/death/interaction states, transitions and IK, environment kits and room dressing, props, lighting, VFX, audio, UI, writing, scripted encounters, and any cinematics. |
| Mech | Chassis and parts, attachment standards, rigs or procedural motion, weapons and projectiles, boost/impact effects, enemy silhouettes and behaviors, arenas, equipment UI/icons, audio, and compatibility across loadouts. |

Count unique assets separately from placements. A single environment kit may
still require many authored rooms. Recoloring an enemy does not automatically
create a new encounter. Mechanical/procedural animation can shift effort toward
programming and tuning; it does not make that work free. For incremental games,
balance iteration and satisfying feedback may dominate the schedule even when
model counts are low. Include capsule art, trailer capture, and other marketing
assets for every candidate.

If a difficult pipeline determines the winner, specify a small timed experiment:
one complete horror locomotion-to-combat sequence, one mech with interchangeable
parts in combat, or one incremental loop with several meaningful upgrade steps.
Such implementation experiments are follow-up work, not assumed completed by
this desk research. Use observed integration and revision time to calibrate the
estimates when available.

**Acceptance:** Identify the five largest asset bottlenecks, their dependencies,
and the change in effort from better AI tooling, simplifying, reusing, or removing each. No asset
family is assumed free merely because code can generate it.

**Output:** A work breakdown and editable scenario model with traceable inputs.

Model the desired horror/mech releases as the main cases, with the archived
compact versions as clearly smaller alternatives. Show the extra asset work,
production time, and possible price/value implications of the larger scopes.
Do not assume revenue grows with content or require all three genres to consume
the same hours. If the desired game takes longer than the comparison horizon,
report that directly rather than shrinking it implicitly or inventing earlier sales.

For each credible release route, split hours/costs into before first paid
availability, between updates/episodes, and through completion/support. Account
for customer support, communication, balance patches, save compatibility, and
release QA within the same 21/31-hour weekly capacity. Check the current
[Steam Early Access guidance](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/earlyaccess)
and relevant packaging rules; staged release is not a presumed workload saving.

Estimate hours before converting to calendar weeks at **21 and 31 hours/week**.
For a later switch, calculate work before and after the switch separately once
its timing is known. Keep all project work inside those hours rather than
treating marketing or asset cleanup as extra free capacity. Include design
iteration, programming, content, animation/art,
audio, tools, integration, saves/settings, accessibility, testing, optimization,
localization scope, marketing, release preparation, and post-release support.
Show dependencies and bottlenecks; a solo developer cannot do every stream in
parallel. Separate a playable prototype, a polished demo, and a saleable release.

For orientation only, 500 hours is about 24 weeks at 21 hours/week or 16 weeks
at 31; 1,000 hours is about 48 or 32 weeks respectively, before interruptions.
These are capacity conversions, not estimates for any of the three games.

Use task-level optimistic/base/pessimistic estimates and explicit rework risks.
Explain where estimates come from: prototype inspection, measured work,
comparable production disclosures, provider information, or judgment. Do not
claim statistical confidence percentiles from guessed ranges.

Model downside, middle, and upside sales scenarios over the same first year
after first paid availability. These are scenarios, not success probabilities. Include an outcome
where revenue does not cover cash costs. Use realistic realized selling prices
after regional mix and discounts, not list price multiplied by an owner count.

Track Early Access and later new buyers separately; existing owners are not
counted as buying the base game again at 1.0. For separately paid episodes, model
later-chapter conversion, new entrants, and bundle discounts with explicit inputs.
For one purchase covering staged chapters, count that purchase once. Include weak
early sales, delays, and the cash/work still required to finish. Do not assume
early revenue finances completion or that successive launches multiply demand.

The calculation should make its deductions explicit:

```text
retained units = units purchased minus refunded/charged-back units
receipts before other costs = retained units
  * average realized price excluding consumption taxes
  * developer share after the store
cash surplus = receipts minus project cash costs and applicable other shares
labor-adjusted surplus = cash surplus minus (owner hours * chosen hourly value)
```

Apply refunds and taxes once. Model publisher recoupment or engine royalties
separately if applicable. Report before personal/company income taxes unless
the relevant situation is supplied. Include store fees, assets, contractors,
software, localization, marketing, and support costs. Validate current terms
against official sources rather than using a universal assumed take-home rate.

Report cash break-even units, units required to compensate the owner's time,
time until first paid release/receipts and full completion, and effective
pre-income-tax earnings per hour.
Because "soonest release" and "most money" may favor different options, show
both first-year-after-first-paid-release earnings and an equal 12/24-month horizon
from the decision date, with support time included. Also show 1.0 and episode
milestones; any first-year-after-1.0 view is a separate, potentially overlapping
observation window, not additional revenue to add. A shorter project should not get automatic
credit for several hypothetical later hits. Show total profit and return on
time separately; do not invent an income goal for the owner.
When net contribution per unit is non-positive, show that no finite sales volume
breaks even under those assumptions. Test lower prices, weaker demand, more
refunds, and longer development. Steam's refund policy is a reason to test short
games' value proposition, not to pad their duration. See [Valve's policy](https://store.steampowered.com/steam_refunds/).

**Acceptance:** Changing art workload, development duration, or sales assumptions
changes the outputs visibly. Gross revenue is never described as personal profit.
A recommendation must disclose which assumptions would reverse it.

before testing. Relevant observations include target players understanding the

price, and the owner's measured content-production speed. Use comparable
exposure and target-audience recruitment if testing concepts against each other.

interest is tested later, track visits, demo use, and wishlists by channel rather
than likes alone; [Valve documents the available wishlist reporting](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/wishlist).

**Acceptance:** State which concept deserves the first prototype, why its
commercial evidence warrants the test, and what could reverse that order.
Give each alternative a status and define what the prototype must demonstrate
about its selling point and representative production workflow. A prototype
choice is not a forecast or full-production approval. After the test, rebuild
the consequential work packages from observed setup, repetition, correction
and integration; remaining content, release and support effort stays explicit.
Player feedback supplies directional evidence, not a reliable sales forecast.
