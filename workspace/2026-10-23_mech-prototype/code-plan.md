# Code plan for the first combat playtest

**Plan updated 22 November 2026 · Movement baseline at `ab5d1fa`**

Build one repeatable fight in **Prototype Map**. The player can already move, jump, dash, aim and fire. Give the existing target health, then let one opponent signal and return fire. Show who took damage, end the fight when either combatant dies, and restart it with one input. The first question is whether maneuvering, tracking and dodging one opponent feels good enough to want another attempt.

**Scope for this implementation.** Complete steps 1–4 in order. Step 2 delivers the first hands-on checkpoint with a damageable target. Step 4 delivers the playable duel with a stationary opponent and retry. Use the existing placeholders, the current straight projectile and a few lines of on-screen text. Aiming remains automatic near the reticle, with free aim when there is no eligible target. Build and playtest all four steps with keyboard and mouse. Keep the existing gamepad bindings and read gameplay input through named Input System actions so the same mechanics can later be played on a controller. [Input priority decision D-010](#/mech-prototype/3)

**Implementation handoff · 22 November 2026.** Step 1 passed in the owner’s hands-on test with ground speed 15 and dash impulse 50. The ring boundary also passed a focused Play Mode check. An offset hitbox exposed a reproducible lock failure. Start step 2 by giving selection, visibility and locked fire one target aim point, then add health and verify actual impacts. Steps 3 and 4 follow once a tester can shoot and destroy the target. Record the observed result and next task here after each step. The first duel uses the existing map and movement as tested. Dash cooldown and airborne thrust are proposed follow-up comparisons.

## What the project already establishes

- **Game direction.** Third-person, Armored Core-inspired mech combat in a roguelike or roguelite. The Workspace favors thruster-driven movement and whole chassis that can share equipment. The hand and back weapon layout is a design reference. Run structure, equipment rules and persistence are open. One chassis and one gun are sufficient to test the controls. [Decisions](#/mech-prototype/3) · [Working brief on chassis and enemies](#/mech-world-concepts/1)
- **Code in the scene.** Six authored gameplay scripts cover a custom capsule motor, Cinemachine-driven third-person camera setup, automatic target selection, held fire, a projectile pool, projectile settings and a damage interface. The scene target has a collider. No authored component receives damage, attacks the player, decides an encounter or starts another attempt. The pool, camera and crosshair references are supplied by the scene.
- **Changes since the October review.** The shallow-ramp clearance fix is in `11087b1`. Four earlier Play Mode checks on a temporary scene copy passed for the 5.7° ramp at 30/60/120 updates per second, a 25 cm step in both directions, ceiling clearance on the ramp, and a flat-ground jump and landing. The reticle now supplies live screen coordinates (`96d6395`). Moving projectile colliders to Ignore Raycast (`df1b108`) also has a passing temporary test covering lock, cover, reacquisition and initial firing direction. Window resizing and projectile damage were outside that test. [Prototype test records](#/mech-prototype/1) · [October review](#/mech-prototype/4)

## Implementation order

### 1. Establish the movement and aim baseline

Use this route again when movement or aiming changes. Cross the ramp and step, jump, air-dash into a corner, jump into a low ceiling and turn the camera near cover. Fire with and without a selected target. Resize the Game view and repeat both shots. The player prefab now sets ground speed to 15 and dash impulse to 50. Keep those values and the existing 0.3 step height for comparison. The owner reported that the route worked and that the revised speeds felt more natural.

**Step 1 passed on keyboard and mouse.** The tester could move, turn, acquire and lose the target, and fire at the visible reticle. Use this route as a regression check when the controller changes. Carry the documented movement and lock cases into project Play Mode tests as those paths are touched.

### 2. Make a shot count

**Lock boundary and first fix.** Keep the current ring calibration. A Play Mode check passed one pixel inside and outside the cutoff on all four sides at 1280 × 720 and 800 × 600. The scene target is a sphere whose hitbox center coincides with its pivot (the GameObject’s origin). In a temporary test copy, the sphere’s hitbox stayed centered under the reticle while its pivot moved to one side. The ring check accepted the hitbox center, but the camera checked visibility along a line to the pivot. That line missed the sphere, so the game dropped the lock in clear sight. Locked shots also aim at the pivot. Use the hitbox center as the aim point for all three operations and resolve the actor that owns the hitbox. Repeat the offset case and the four edge checks. The offset target should lock and take a hit, and a wall should still break lock. [Prototype test records](#/mech-prototype/1)

Implement `Health : IDamageable` on the target. Clamp health at zero, publish a change after damage and emit death once. Give the aim query and the weapon one target owner and one world-space aim point. A child collider can resolve its owning health through a parent or a simple hitbox reference. Give each projectile its shooter and an explicit set of valid collision layers. Keep the muzzle as the start of projectile travel so a wall between gun and target still stops the shot. Use plain text to show health. Restarting Play Mode is sufficient to reset this first target.

**Shot behavior.** The camera selects the intended point. The moving projectile determines the hit. If the camera can see around cover but the muzzle is blocked, the round stops at the cover. Skip the shooter's colliders and other projectiles, then resolve the first valid world or actor hit along the traveled segment. A straight round returns to the pool after that hit. On reuse, set its owner and shot data from the new firing request.

**First hands-on test.** With a temporary 30 HP target and the existing 10-damage shot, three unobstructed hits should take it to zero. Shoot from inside the 20-unit lock range, then use free aim from beyond it. Repeat with an offset child hitbox, behind cover and while firing across another projectile. One hit changes health once, cover blocks damage, and a dead target drops out of selection. Ask the tester to strafe and dash while tracking it. Write down when the reticle, impact and intended target disagree.

### 3. Add one opponent that can be dodged

Reuse `Health` on the player. Extract weapon cooldown and projectile launch from `ShootingController` behind a small `TryFire` method. Player input supplies an aim point from the reticle or lock. The first enemy stays in place and requests an attack against the player.

Give the attack component a C# state machine. During **Wind-up** it signals the shot and commits its aim. During **Fire** it calls the shared weapon once. After **Recovery** it reports completion and can accept another request. The caller can request the attack, observe completion and cancel it. Death or the end of the encounter cancels a pending attack and clears its cue. The weapon owns its firing cooldown. Set wind-up and recovery durations in serialized fields on the attack component. [Decision D-009](#/mech-prototype/3)

**First playtest setup.** Give the enemy a fixed starting position within the player's lock range and a clear initial shot, with existing cover nearby. Start a wind-up when the player is visible and the weapon is ready. Show the cue and capture the player's position at that moment. Aim the shot at that captured point even if the player dashes or reaches cover. Once recovery finishes, look for the next clear shot. This gives testers a consistent attack to learn. Aim commitment and the two durations are playtest defaults.

**Ready to proceed when** standing in the shot costs player health, a timed dash can avoid it, and cover protects either combatant. Show both health values. Fire both guns from the same pool and verify their cooldowns are independent. Reuse a player projectile for an enemy shot and confirm it can damage the player while ignoring its new shooter. Let one wind-up finish and verify that exactly one shot launches before recovery. Kill the enemy during its next wind-up and verify that the cue ends and the pending shot never fires.

### 4. Close the encounter and play it repeatedly

Give a scene-local encounter controller three states called **Fighting**, **Won** and **Lost**. It listens to the two death events, resolves one outcome, stops combat and exposes Retry. Prototype Map can start and finish a fight on its own. A future run controller can start that same encounter and consume its result when there is a carryover rule to test. Reload the scene for a fresh attempt. Include a keyboard retry action and a visible control hint. Build the Windows player from the tested revision and run it outside the Editor.

**End-of-fight rule for this playtest.** The first lethal hit processed while Fighting fixes the result. Enemy death wins the duel, and player death loses it. Cancel pending attacks and stop applying projectile damage as soon as the result is set, including later impacts in the same frame. Keep the result and retry hint visible. Test both outcomes with another shot already in flight, then retry and confirm the new encounter accepts damage normally.

**First combat playtest is ready when** a tester can win, deliberately lose and retry in the player without opening the Editor. Repeat with shots in flight at the end of a fight. Both actors must return to their starting positions and health. Check that cooldowns reset, targeting starts fresh, old shots disappear and each death produces one result. Replay the same setup several times before changing a variable.

## Architecture for this prototype

- **Component composition.** Give the player and enemy their own health and weapon components. Player input and enemy attack logic can then drive the same weapon code. Only the player needs a camera and reticle.
- **Composition Root and manual dependency injection.** Use the Prototype Map scene setup as the place that connects the duel. Assign each weapon its projectile pool and the HUD the actors it observes through Inspector references. If enemies later spawn at runtime, wire their spawner here and let it pass the target and pool to each new enemy. Keep win, loss and retry in the encounter controller. If setup is repeated across encounters, evaluate Reflex at this wiring point so gameplay components can keep receiving their dependencies directly. [Composition Root by Mark Seemann](https://blog.ploeh.dk/2011/07/28/CompositionRoot/)
- **Use ScriptableObject architecture (SOAP) selectively.** Treat the existing `BulletType` as a shared definition for base damage and speed. Two guns can use the same definition while keeping independent cooldowns. Each actor owns its current health, and the pool owns in-flight projectile state. [Unity ScriptableObject manual](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.5/manual/scripting/fundamental-unity-types/class-scriptable-object)
- **Strategy when firing rules diverge.** Changing projectile speed or damage only requires tuning. If a later weapon charges on press and fires on release, extract the firing rule into an interchangeable behavior. Both rules can still launch shots through the existing projectile pool.
- **Observer for local notifications.** A projectile hit calls `IDamageable.TakeDamage` directly. Each actor’s health raises typed C# events such as `HealthChanged` and `Died`. The HUD and encounter controller listen to the actors they track and unsubscribe when disabled. Consider a ScriptableObject event channel when independently loaded systems need the same notification, for example if music and UI in different scenes both respond to the end of a fight.
- **Start with one HUD component and defer Model–View–Presenter (MVP).** Health owns HP, targeting owns the lock, and the encounter controller decides the outcome and handles retry. The HUD reads their initial values, listens for changes and updates the widgets. At this stage a separate presenter would mostly forward those same values. Extract one when there is a presentation rule worth testing on its own, such as which warning takes priority, or when a second HUD needs the same display logic.
- **Finite state machines for attack and encounter flow.** The attack advances through wind-up, fire and recovery. The encounter independently tracks fighting, won or lost. Introduce a Unity Behavior graph when playtests need the enemy to choose among firing, waiting for a clear shot or moving sideways to regain sight. The graph can request an attack, observe completion and cancel it. The attack component still owns its timing. Evaluate a third-party tree only if a concrete requirement emerges, such as resuming an enemy midway through an encounter. [Unity Behavior for Unity 6000.5](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.5/manual/packages-list/packages-all/pack-safe/com-unity-behavior)

## What the playtest decides

For each attempt, note the input device, outcome, approximate duration and one concrete moment the tester found satisfying or confusing. Ask whether they could keep the enemy in view during a dash, predict when to evade, and explain why a shot hit or missed. Capture the simplest reproduction for a problem. Use Edit Mode tests for damage arithmetic and single death notification. Use Play Mode tests for hit, cover and retry behavior alongside the movement fixtures.

**Gamepad pass after the keyboard-and-mouse duel.** The current controller normalizes movement input, so half stick deflection gives full directional input. It also treats a held right stick like a mouse delta. When gamepad playtesting begins, preserve left-stick magnitude, interpret right-stick look as a turn rate over time, and keep mouse look based on pointer delta. Test half and full stick travel and a one-second turn at 30, 60 and 120 FPS. Then run the same duel through retry with a gamepad and recheck mouse look.

**Mobility experiments proposed on 22 November.** Establish the repeatable duel first. Keep the opponent, map, ground speed and dash impulse fixed while comparing one change at a time.

1. **Dash cooldown.** Try a tunable delay after a dash actually fires. An early press gives no extra impulse and leaves the timer alone. Show readiness and remaining cooldown on the HUD. Compare repeated dodges against the same enemy wind-up. Can the tester tell when the next dash is available, and does choosing when to use it make the shot more interesting?
2. **Airborne thrust.** Keep tap-to-jump. While airborne, holding the existing Jump action (Space) applies upward thrust, including when held through takeoff. Releasing it restores normal gravity. As a first comparison, try a short per-flight thrust allowance that refills on landing. Show remaining thrust and active use on the HUD. Check short taps, releasing and reapplying in midair, depletion, ceiling contact, landing and retry. Can the player track the target while climbing, and does landing create a meaningful opening? Duration and refill are trial settings.

The movement component owns the timers and exposes their state to the existing HUD. Retry starts both abilities fresh. Keep Prototype Map for these comparisons. If a ceiling or lack of cover prevents a particular test, first try another spot or a single placeholder obstacle. Revisit the map once a specific layout limitation is repeatable.

If camera or control problems dominate, repeat the same fight after changing one thing. If players can read the wind-up but find tracking too easy, try a short sideways movement before the shot. If taking cover changes what players expect the enemy to do, test whether it should finish its committed shot, wait or reposition. That choice is the first candidate for a behavior graph. When the duel holds up and the question becomes what makes a second fight interesting, try two encounters using the same arena with one explicit carryover rule.

Procedural rooms, rewards, permanent progression, saves, chassis selection, four-weapon loadouts and energy, heat or stagger systems each answer later design questions. The first milestone ends when a tester can fight, retry, and tell us what should change next.
